AI, without the hype — where to actually begin

You've heard enough about AI. This page is the short version: one thing to do today, real uses for your line of work, a handful of good free courses and channels, prompts you can copy and paste, and the few rules that keep you out of trouble. Nothing here is paid or sponsored, and no prior tech background is assumed.

The 20-minute start

Do this before you read a single article about AI. The skill comes from using it on something real, not from studying it.

  1. Make one account.Pick ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini — any of them. The free tier is plenty. Add it to your phone's home screen so it's one tap away.
  2. Give it a real job.Not "tell me about AI." Take something you were going to do anyway — an email you've been putting off, a document you need to understand, a plan you need to lay out — and paste it in with a clear ask.
  3. Fix its answer.Read the draft and tell it what's wrong: "too formal," "shorter," "you got the price wrong." That back-and-forth is the skill. Nobody gets it right in one shot.
One sentence to remember: it's a fast, tireless first-draft machine with no judgment of its own — you supply the judgment. That's the whole job.

What it is, and what it isn't

Good at

  • Turning rough notes into clean writing
  • Summarizing a long document or email chain
  • Comparing two options side by side
  • Making a first-version plan or checklist
  • Explaining anything at any level of detail
  • Reading a photo of handwriting or a page
  • Being endlessly patient while you practice

Not reliable for

  • Exact numbers, prices, or dates it wasn't handed
  • Current local rules — codes, permits, licensing
  • Legal, tax, medical, or safety decisions
  • Knowing what it doesn't know (it won't say "I'm guessing")
  • Anything you'd sign without reading

Pick your lane

Same tool, different uses. Tap the one closest to your work or your life.

Project manager, high-end remodels — the highest-value uses first.

  • Bids, apples to apples. Paste two subs' bids and ask what's included, what's excluded, and what to clarify before signing. It catches scope gaps you'd otherwise find in month three.
  • Client updates that don't eat your evening. Dump the week's facts in bullet form; get a warm, professional update you edit for 60 seconds.
  • Change orders. Turn a verbal scope change into clear written language, then ask "what am I leaving ambiguous that could bite me later?"
  • Spec books and inspection reports. Paste a section, ask what's missing, vague, or conflicts with another section.
  • Sequencing and pre-construction. "Here's the scope and the site constraints — build me a rough sequence and list what typically goes wrong at each stage, and what to lock down first."
  • Punch lists and handoff. Photo of handwritten notes → typed, organized, room by room.
  • Hard conversations, rehearsed. Have it play the upset client or the defensive sub, then critique your replies.
Codes and permits: use it to prepare questions, never as the answer. The AHJ and your licensed trades are the answer.

Selling, listing, and keeping clients warm.

  • Listing descriptions that don't sound like every other listing — give it the house's real character, not just the bedroom count.
  • Follow-up that happens. Turn a showing into a specific, personal next-touch message in seconds.
  • Objection practice. Have it argue the buyer's or seller's side so you've heard it before you hear it for real.
  • Neighborhood and market write-ups — it drafts, you fact-check every number against the MLS.
  • Client education. "Explain closing costs to a nervous first-time buyer in plain English, 200 words."

For teachers, parents, and homework help.

  • Lesson plans and quizzes in minutes, then adapted to your actual class.
  • Explain it three ways — "like I'm 8," "like I'm 15," "like I'm a busy adult" — until it lands.
  • Practice problems with answer keys, and instant re-explanation when a kid gets stuck.
  • Plain-language drafts of letters home, permission slips, and progress notes.
  • Study guides built from a photo of the textbook page or the teacher's handout.
For kids: sit with them at first. The goal is a kid who can check the AI's work, not a kid who trusts it.

Admin, marketing, and everything that eats a workday.

  • Email triage. Paste a messy thread: "what's actually being asked of me, and what do I owe back?"
  • Meeting notes → action items. Decisions made, who owes what by when, open questions.
  • Documents and policies. "Summarize this, flag anything vague, tell me what I should ask before signing."
  • Spreadsheet help. Describe the sheet; get the formula and what can go wrong with it.
  • Marketing copy, job posts, SOPs — first drafts you polish, not finished work.

The everyday stuff, which is honestly where most people get hooked.

  • Plan a trip with real constraints — budget, mobility, days off — and ask what you're overlooking.
  • Meals for the week from what's already in the fridge and what's on sale.
  • Letters and disputes. Insurance, warranty, a refund a business is stonewalling — get a firm, clear letter.
  • Learn anything. Pick a subject and ask it to teach you in 20-minute chunks, quizzing you as you go.
  • Prepare for appointments. Organize your symptoms, questions, and history so you use the doctor's 15 minutes well.

Five rules of the road

  • Assume it's confidently wrong until checked. It sounds exactly as sure when it's making something up. Verify numbers, prices, dates, names, rules, and anything you'd act on.
  • Don't paste private stuff into free tools. Client names, addresses, phone numbers, bids and pricing, medical records, account numbers, passwords. Describe the situation instead — "a $180k remodel in a 1950s house" works just as well. Turn off "improve the model for everyone" in settings if you can.
  • You're the editor. A first draft from AI plus your judgment is a win. A first draft sent unread is how people get embarrassed.
  • It never makes the final call on code, permits, contracts, taxes, medical, or safety. Those have humans and agencies attached for good reason.
  • Free is enough to start. Pay ($20/month-ish, or a "Pro" plan) only once you're hitting usage limits or genuinely using it daily.

Questions everyone asks

Which one should I pick?

Any of them. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are all capable at everyday work, and the gap between them matters far less than how well you brief them. Pick one, stay with it two weeks, and stop switching. If a friend already uses one, use that one — then you have somebody to swap tricks with.

Do I need to be technical, or good at "prompting"?

No. The whole trick is context: say who you are, what the real situation is, paste the actual document, and state what a good answer looks like. People who write the vaguest requests get the worst results, and they blame the tool.

Is it going to take my job?

It's taking over tasks, not judgment. The uncomfortable truth is that the person who uses it well will out-produce the person who refuses to. Which is the argument for being the first one in your shop or your office to actually get good at it.

Phone or computer?

Both, and you don't need to choose. Phone is for quick asks, photos, and voice — you can literally talk to it instead of typing. Desktop is better for long documents and anything you'll format or print.

Is what I type private?

It depends entirely on the plan and the service's settings — free tiers commonly reserve the right to review or train on your chats, paid business plans usually don't. Assume anything you paste into a free tool could be read by a stranger, and check the privacy setting before you paste something sensitive.

How do I actually get good at this?

Ten minutes a day on your real work beats a five-hour course. Use it for something you were doing anyway: today's hardest email, this week's plan, that document you keep avoiding. In two weeks you'll know more than most people who talk about AI all day.
